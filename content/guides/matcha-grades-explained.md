Shop for matcha and you will meet the same four words again and again: ceremonial, premium, daily and culinary. They sound like an official ladder. They are not. As far as we can tell, Japan has no single legal grading standard for matcha, so each seller decides what the words mean.

## What the words usually signal

| Label | Usually sold for | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Ceremonial | Whisked with water and drunk straight | Origin, harvest year, and notes naming the farm or cultivar |
| Premium / daily | Everyday cups and lattes | One ingredient, a named origin |
| Culinary | Baking, smoothies, ice cream | Fine for cooking, usually too bitter for sipping |

## Why the labels drift

Because nobody enforces them, "ceremonial" can be printed on a very good tin and on an average one. A higher price tells you what the seller wants to charge, not what is inside.

## What to look at instead

Start with the [label](/guides/how-to-read-a-matcha-label/): a single ingredient, a named origin and a date. Then match the tin to how you will use it. If you add milk and sweetener, a daily-grade tin is usually the sensible buy. If you drink it plain, spend more time on the origin and harvest date than on the grade word.

## A word on prices

Matcha prices have risen in recent years, so a tin that used to be a bargain may not be one now. We will publish the figures and sources in a separate guide rather than quote them here.
