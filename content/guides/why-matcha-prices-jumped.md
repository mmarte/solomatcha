Matcha drinkers have noticed two major changes on store shelves and online shops: prices per tin have climbed, and popular options frequently go out of stock. 

This price movement is not random. It is the result of rapid demand growth colliding with tight supply constraints in Japan. To understand why a 30-gram tin costs significantly more now than it did a few years ago, you have to look at how raw tea leaves are grown, traded, and exported.

## The Raw Material: Tencha

Matcha begins as tencha. Tencha refers to green tea leaves that are shaded before harvest, steamed, dried flat without being rolled, and destemmed. These dried leaf flakes are later ground into the fine powder sold as matcha.

Tencha cannot simply be produced on demand from any tea bush. Shading the fields requires specialized canopies or screens. Processing the harvested leaf requires dedicated tencha drying ovens, which are distinct from the rolling machinery used for standard green teas like sencha.

Because tencha requires specific farming setups and processing infrastructure, supply cannot expand overnight. When wholesale demand spikes, tea producers cannot instantly turn standard tea acreage into high-grade tencha fields.

## Wholesale Tencha Costs Spiked

The bottleneck in tencha production became acute between 2024 and 2026. Wholesale raw leaf auctions saw historic cost increases.

Kyoto wholesale tencha prices rose by roughly 170% from 2024 to 2025. This steep increase hit tea blenders and merchants across Japan. Kyoto tea company Ippodo issued a public notice on August 1, 2025, addressing these cost pressures. The company reported that tencha costs had reached about 2.7 times the Kyoto rate seen in May 2025. In response to the supply shortage, Ippodo instituted purchase limits on its products.

The upward pressure continued into the following spring harvest. A Japanese news report indicated that the average tencha price in April 2026 reached 8,235 yen per kg. That figure was about 1.7 times the price recorded the previous year.

When raw tencha prices double or triple at regional auctions, packagers and exporters have little choice but to raise the final price of their packaged matcha.

## Export Demand Rose Rapidly

While raw tencha production remained physically constrained, international demand increased sharply.

The United States has become the primary destination for Japanese exports of powdered green tea. In 2024, 78% of Japan's powdered green tea exports went directly to the US. According to retail data from NIQ, US retail matcha sales grew 86% over a three-year span.

This surge in overseas retail and food-service consumption drew heavily on Japan's existing tea supply. Matcha makes up only 7 to 8% of total tea production in Japan. However, matcha accounted for about half of the 8,798 tonnes of green tea that Japan exported in 2024. 

When a category that represents less than a tenth of national output suddenly accounts for half of all exports, domestic processors face intense inventory pressure. Buyers from abroad competed directly against traditional domestic markets for the same annual harvest.

## Production Bottlenecks

Expanding matcha production takes time. Shaded tea fields need careful soil management and specialized shading structures. In addition, tencha grinding requires slow, precision stone mills to avoid overheating the leaf during milling.

Even if a farm decides to convert standard open-air tea rows to shaded tencha production, the transition requires capital investment and several growing cycles to achieve quality results. Consequently, supply could not match the pace of the export market.

With inventory depleted, vendors faced two options: raise prices to cover the higher auction costs of tencha, or cap orders. Many did both. Purchase limits became common on vendor websites to prevent single buyers or commercial operations from clearing out inventory intended for retail customers.

## Trade and Tariff Changes

Import policies also shifted during this period. For buyers in the United States, import costs initially included tariffs on green tea shipments. 

One vendor, O-cha, reported that a 15% US tariff on tea under HTS code 0902.10 was removed on November 13, 2025. While tariff relief can lower the administrative cost of bringing tea into the US, the fundamental wholesale cost of the raw leaves still dictates the baseline shelf price. The underlying cost of tencha remains elevated due to the imbalance between supply and demand.

## What This Means for Shoppers

Higher prices make buying decisions more critical. Because pure matcha is sold in small quantities (often 20 g to 40 g tins), price jumps per gram add up quickly. 

Shoppers should be aware that packaging terms do not always indicate quality. Japan has no formal matcha grading guideline defined by law. Terms such as "ceremonial grade" are marketing descriptors rather than standardized legal definitions. Until tencha acreage and processing capacity catch up with international demand, wholesale prices are likely to keep retail matcha tins at higher price points than buyers saw in previous years.
