The five-second label test

Five things to check before you buy a tin

Most bad matcha is easy to spot from the packaging. Run these checks on any product page or shelf label.

  • One ingredient

    The list should say matcha and nothing else. Sugar, milk powder, creamer or “natural flavors” mean you are buying a latte mix.

  • A named origin

    Look for a place such as Uji, Nishio, Yame or Kagoshima. “Imported” or “Product of Asia” tells you nothing.

  • A harvest year

    Matcha fades and flattens with age. A seller who prints the harvest or best-by date is keeping track of stock.

  • A vivid green color

    Fresh matcha is usually bright, slightly cool green. Yellow or brown powder points to old leaf or a lower-quality blend.

  • A fine, silky texture

    Good matcha is milled very fine and whisks smooth. Gritty powder that clumps hard is a warning sign.

Grades

“Ceremonial” is a seller’s word

Japan has no single legal grading scale for matcha. Sellers use the same few labels with different meanings, so judge the tin, not the badge.

LabelUsually sold forWhat to check
CeremonialWhisked with water and drunk straightOrigin, harvest year, and tasting notes from a seller who names the cultivar or farm.
Daily / premiumEveryday cups and lattesA single ingredient and a stated origin. This is the best-value band for most people.
CulinaryBaking, smoothies, ice creamFine for cooking. It tastes stronger and more bitter, so it is a poor choice for sipping.

Rule of thumb: a higher price does not prove a better tin, and a low price on a large pouch usually means older or blended leaf.

Why it costs more

Demand grew faster than the leaf supply

Matcha comes from tencha, shaded tea leaf that takes years of farm capacity to expand. Three figures explain the squeeze.

Some large Japanese producers have stopped taking new wholesale customers and cap how much each retail customer can buy. Expect sold-out tins and price changes between harvests.
